01 / THE QUESTIONFROM INTUITION TO EVIDENCE

Can we predict
what makes a
model better?

More data brings credibility. The harder question is how quickly a model improves, and when further complexity stops paying.

This study predicts claim frequency: how often claims occur. The inputs are policy records with driver characteristics, vehicle details and earned exposure. Most records have a zero claim count, so a model must learn useful relationships from sparse outcomes.

The experiment grows the training set, compares model sizes, and measures performance on unseen records. It asks which models keep improving with scale, how much each increase buys, and where a larger network simply adds cost.

↓ONE ENSEMBLE PREDICTION
＋ What does Poisson deviance tell us?THE METRIC, EXPLAINED

Each model predicts a claim rate. Multiplying that rate by earned exposure gives an expected claim count. Poisson deviance measures how well those expected counts fit the observed counts on unseen records. Lower is better.

A drop from 0.29200 to 0.28885 is about a 1.1% relative improvement in this loss. It is a measure of predictive fit; it does not directly mean 1.1% more profit or 1.1% lower premiums.

Expected claims = exposure × predicted claim rate

01 / THE SMALL-DATA TRAP

The same bigger model can hurt, then help.

At 5% data, TabM small scores 0.29389 and TabM large scores 0.29681: the larger model is worse. At full data, large reaches 0.28984 against small’s 0.29066. More capacity becomes useful when there is enough signal to learn from.

02 / THE NEAR-FLAT LAST STEP

More than twice the parameters. Almost the same loss.

At full data, standard TabM xlarge has roughly 2.23 times as many trainable parameters as large, yet its deviance is only about 0.0000066 lower. Both round to 0.28984. Later TabM-mini extensions improve further, so this is a local plateau, not a hard limit.

03 / A LARGER TRANSFORMER

Size alone does not settle the ranking.

At full data, the vanilla Transformer’s medium model scores 0.29085. The large model scores 0.29094, slightly worse despite roughly three times the parameters. A smooth fitted frontier does not mean every larger configuration wins.

These bars show the share of the original gap removed in each step. The resources keep doubling; the increments in improvement shrink. Here, pp means percentage points: one pp removes one of the starting 100 gap units.

In the main-sweep TabM data fit, one doubling removes 19.3% of the starting gap. A parameter doubling removes 9.7% under the separate full-data parameter fit.

D uses the main-sweep family envelope, whose winning size can change. P uses the full-data Pareto set: configurations that improve on smaller choices. These are separate descriptive fits, not a controlled comparison of two investments or a formula for changing both resources at once. L∞ = 0.28749 is an empirical fitted floor. The 1×–16× curves illustrate the fitted equations; they are not additional observations or validated extrapolations.

Published exponents and calculated effects of doubling
Why are there two TabM data exponents? ＋

The main-sweep TabM data exponent is 0.309, giving about 19.3% less reducible loss per doubling. Add the largest model trained only at full data and the best-achieved exponent rises to 0.409, giving about 24.7%. That extra point changes the fitted curve and weakens its fit quality. Both figures belong to the paper; they describe different selections of results.

THE RESOURCE TRADE-OFF

Better fit has a cost.

The best full-data result also uses the most compute. These representative configurations show why loss alone cannot decide which model to deploy.

Going from TabM-mini xlarge-2 to gigantic uses 3.85 times as many parameters and about four times the computation per epoch. Test deviance falls by a further 0.00018. The gain continues, but the resource commitment grows much faster.

08 / WHAT IT MEANSAN EMPIRICAL GUIDE, WITH BOUNDARIES

Choose the model
for the data you have.

The useful question is how well a model turns your available data and compute into predictive improvement.

↗

Start with a credible baseline.
Measure the learning curve.
Scale what demonstrably works.

What the evidence supports −

Architecture-specific scaling regularities on this motor frequency task. Stronger TabM scaling at large data sizes. A useful connection between modern learning curves and the actuarial idea of credibility.

What the experiment does not establish ＋

Universal model rankings, future-period performance, or a guaranteed pricing or profit uplift. The study uses a single anonymized portfolio and an IID split, not a temporal evaluation. Gradient boosting is outside the main comparison. Recipes were selected by experts without systematic hyperparameter optimization; broader tuning may change the rankings.

How to read the winning curves ＋

Each family curve selects the lowest test loss across model sizes. It describes the best observed envelope, not one fixed model or an unbiased estimate of a future model-selection process. The largest TabM-mini and SSL variants were run only at full data.

How to read the self-supervision result ＋

The SSL recipe improves larger Transformer-family results, but its width and depth schedule also differs. This is evidence about the whole recipe; it does not isolate the causal effect of the auxiliary loss alone.

How to read the compute results ＋

The table reports one-seed FLOPs per epoch. An equivalent five-seed ensemble proxy multiplies that cost by five. Neither measures total training cost: early stopping changes the number of epochs, so an exact total-compute optimum is not established.
